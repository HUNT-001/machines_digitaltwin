How It Works

Stator

The stationary outer core is built from thin, insulated silicon-steel laminations to curb eddy-current loss. Three sets of copper windings sit in its slots, displaced 120° apart in space, and are fed from the 415 V, 3-phase, 50 Hz supply through R, Y, B terminals.

Rotating Magnetic Field

Three currents that are 120° apart in time, flowing in windings 120° apart in space, sum vectorially to a magnetic field of constant magnitude that sweeps around the bore at synchronous speed.

Ns = 120·f / P

Induced EMF (Faraday's Law)

The rotating flux cuts the closed rotor bars/windings, inducing an EMF exactly as a moving magnet induces EMF in a stationary coil.

e = −N · dΦ/dt

Rotor Current & Torque

The induced EMF drives current through the closed rotor circuit (via slip rings, brushes, and the external rheostat). This current, reacting with the stator flux, produces the torque that turns the shaft — always slightly slower than the field, since relative motion is needed to induce anything at all.

s = (Ns − N) / Ns

Slip Rings & Brushes

Because this is a wound rotor (not squirrel-cage), its three winding ends are brought out to three copper slip rings on the shaft. Stationary carbon brushes ride on these rings, letting an external rheostat be inserted in the rotor circuit — the basis of the rotor-rheostat speed control method used on this bench.
